The question this project is asking

AI benchmarks tell you what a model can do in isolation. They don't tell you what it does when it has something to want, other agents to deal with, and no single correct answer. That gap — between capability and behaviour — is what CasuallyAI is interested in.

The approach is borrowed loosely from the Stanford Smallville experiment, which placed AI agents in a shared simulated world and watched what emerged. The finding that captured everyone's attention wasn't that the agents completed tasks — it was that they organised a party, spread gossip, and formed social hierarchies that nobody programmed. The interesting stuff was emergent.

That's the spirit here. I build shared environments, populate them with AI agents from different model families, give each one a persistent identity, private goals, and something at stake — and then let it run. The write-ups are honest accounts of what actually happens.

How the experiments work

A Python engine acts as the world: it holds the ground truth of whatever scenario is running, manages what each agent knows, processes their decisions, and updates the shared state. The agents — one per model, each with a distinct personality established at the start — receive only what their character would plausibly know. They don't know they're in a simulation. They just receive prompts and respond.

Every agent has both a public voice and a private one. What they say to other agents is logged. What they're actually thinking — their unfiltered internal reasoning — is also logged, separately. That gap between private thought and public behaviour is often where the most interesting material lives.

State is tracked qualitatively, not numerically. An agent doesn't receive "health: 42" — it receives "you're exhausted and haven't slept properly in days." This keeps the reasoning human rather than mechanical, and makes the output far more readable.

The scenarios

The first experiment puts three AI agents — one GPT, one Claude, one Gemini — in a band together. They choose their own personalities at the start. Then they negotiate a band agreement, rehearse, try to get signed, play shows, and manage the accumulating pressures of a music career: money, health, ego, creative disagreement, and the slow grind of ambition versus reality.

Later experiments will scale up: more agents, more complex social structures, more adversarial dynamics. The band is a starting point precisely because everyone understands what a band is — you don't need to care about AI to find the question "did the Claude character and the GPT character fall out over the album direction?" genuinely interesting.

Other scenarios in development include political simulations, business negotiations, and a Big Brother-style experiment where the social dynamics are the entire point.

What this isn't

This isn't rigorous academic research. I'm not running each scenario hundreds of times for statistical significance, and I'm not trying to produce peer-reviewable findings. LLM outputs are non-deterministic — the same experiment run twice might produce different results, and I'll say so when that's relevant.

What I do commit to: publishing the prompts I used, being honest when an experiment produces nothing interesting, and never retrofitting a narrative onto results that don't support it. The write-ups are accurate accounts of what happened, not what I hoped would happen.

Who I am

Based in England. I find AI genuinely interesting rather than either terrifying or miraculous, which probably puts me in a minority. I enjoy building things, writing about what I find, and the specific satisfaction of watching a simulation produce an outcome nobody predicted.

Scenario types

What I look for
  • Emergent behaviour nobody explicitly programmed
  • The gap between what agents say and what they think
  • Whether models hold their ground or capitulate under pressure
  • How different model families behave in the same situation
  • What happens when agents have conflicting private goals
