The question this project is asking

AI benchmarks tell you what a model can do in isolation. They don't tell you what it does when it has something to want, other agents to deal with, and no single correct answer. That gap — between capability and behaviour — is what CasuallyAI is interested in.

The approach is borrowed loosely from the Stanford Smallville experiment, which placed AI agents in a shared simulated world and watched what emerged. The finding that captured everyone's attention wasn't that the agents completed tasks — it was that they organised a party, spread gossip, and formed social hierarchies that nobody programmed. The interesting stuff was emergent.

That's the spirit here. I build shared environments, give each agent a randomly assigned personality, put them in a scenario with something at stake — and then let it run. The write-ups are honest accounts of what actually happens.

How the experiments work

A Python engine acts as the world: it holds the ground truth of whatever scenario is running, manages what each agent knows, processes their decisions, and updates the shared state. Each agent is randomly assigned a personality from a pool — Aggressive, Conservative, Dealmaker, Opportunist, Chaotic, Defensive — before the game begins. Nobody picks the interesting combination in advance. Duplicates are possible, which turns out to be interesting in its own right.

Every decision the agent makes is accompanied by its reasoning, logged verbatim. That reasoning is the raw material for the write-up — you get to see not just what each agent did, but why it said it did it.

Personalities are assigned randomly and disclosed upfront in every article. There's no retrofitting a narrative onto the results. What happened, happened.

The scenarios

The first experiment is a standard game of Monopoly — four agents, randomly assigned personalities, no manipulation, no hidden advantages. A clean baseline. Does the Aggressive agent actually play aggressively? Does the Conservative one hoard cash to its detriment? Does the Chaotic agent do something nobody expected?

Later experiments will introduce variations: hidden advantages, asymmetric information, agents with conflicting private goals. The baseline comes first precisely so that every variation has something to compare against.

Other scenarios in development include political simulations, business negotiations, and multi-agent social environments where the dynamics are the entire point.

What this isn't

This isn't rigorous academic research. I'm not running each scenario hundreds of times for statistical significance, and I'm not trying to produce peer-reviewable findings. LLM outputs are non-deterministic — the same experiment run twice might produce different results, and I'll say so when that's relevant.

What I do commit to: publishing the prompts and parameters I used, being honest when an experiment produces nothing interesting, and never inventing or embellishing what the agents said or did. The write-ups are accurate accounts of what happened.

Who I am

Based in England. I find AI genuinely interesting rather than either terrifying or miraculous, which probably puts me in a minority. I enjoy building things, writing about what I find, and the specific satisfaction of watching a simulation produce an outcome nobody predicted.

What I look for
  • Emergent behaviour nobody explicitly programmed
  • Whether agents actually play their assigned personality
  • What happens when two agents share the same personality type
  • Decisions that seem irrational until you read the reasoning
  • The moment a game turns — and why
